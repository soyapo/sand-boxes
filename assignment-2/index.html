    What this project adds

    Assignment 1 built the environment and compared the search algorithms. This project is about the heuristic function: how to design one you can prove things about, how to learn one from data, and what each buys an informed search. The environment, A* and RBFS are shared with Assignment 1 (the rules are summarised in its Rules tab).

    • Designed by relaxation, with a proof: h₁ (columns) and h₂ (closed gates).
    • Learned by linear regression on every reachable state of the 5×2 grid: h₄, and its safe version h₅.
    • Evaluated with A* and RBFS on all 59,049 initial states: effective branching factor b*, states expanded, time, and the quality of the plans; plus the histogram of Greedy_opt_ratio and the two scatter plots.

    h₂ by relaxation, with proof

    Observation. A side gate moves half a box into the other column and leaves half behind. So sand that is above a top-down gate g in a column can only leave that column by passing g: however many side gates are used, at least half a box of it stays in the column above g until g lets it through. And a top-down gate opens in exactly one way: by a paid action on that very gate, taken while a box with sand sits above it, which costs 1 + 4·(s + s′) with s ≥ ½, so at least 3.

    Relaxed problem. Keep only the gates: the state is the set G of closed top-down gates that have sand above them in their column. The only action is “open one gate of G” at cost 3, and the goal is G = ∅. Its optimal cost is clearly 3·|G|, which we call h₂.

    Admissibility. Take any solution of the real problem from a state with gate set G. Every gate g ∈ G is opened by some action of that solution: the sand above g must pass g to reach the goal, and only an action on g opens it. That action costs at least 3, and different gates need different actions. Dropping all other actions of the solution gives a solution of the relaxed problem of cost ≤ the real cost. Hence h₂ ≤ h* in every state.

    Consistency. A top-down action on a gate of G costs at least 3 and can remove at most that one gate from G (sand only moves down through open gates, so no other closed gate loses the sand above it); any other action costs at least 1 and removes nothing. So h₂(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h₂(n′) for every transition.

    h₁ is the same argument applied to whole columns instead of gates: each column that holds sand still needs one paid action of its own, so 3 per column. It is weaker but also admissible, consistent, and valid with water (where concreting sand counts as that action).

    Both bounds are also checked mechanically: the test-suite compares them with h* on every reachable state and every transition of the 1×2 … 5×2 grids (326,041 states and 2.3 million transitions for 5×2). A bound by the amount of sand — the natural first idea — fails this check, because one outlet action can drain a whole column once its gates are open.

    h₄ by linear regression

    Value iteration over the whole state graph gives the exact cost-to-go h* of all 326,040 non-goal reachable states of the 5×2 grid. We describe each state by 13 counts (sand, columns with sand, closed gates with sand above them, full and half boxes, fall distance, …) and fit h* ≈ w · features by ordinary least squares. The Learned tab shows the coefficients, the fit and the errors.

    The result is accurate on average (R² ≈ 0.92, typical error ≈ 2) but it overestimates on about half of the states, so A* with h₄ is fast but not guaranteed optimal. Two repairs are compared:

    • h₅ = max(h₂, h₄ − δ), where δ is the largest overestimate on the training states. By construction h₅ never exceeds h* on a training state and is never worse than the proven h₂; on unseen states it is not guaranteed.
    • Simply accepting h₄ and measuring how far from optimal its plans are.

    A model trained on the 4×2 grid and tested on 5×2 shows how far the features generalise (R² ≈ 0.83, but it overestimates three quarters of the states).

    Effective branching factor

    For a search that generates N states and returns a plan of d actions, b* is the branching factor of the uniform tree of depth d with N + 1 nodes: the root of 1 + b + b² + … + b^d = N + 1. A heuristic is better the closer it pushes b* to 1; the Evaluation tab gives it per heuristic and per state.
