    What this project adds

    Assignments 1 and 2 searched the state space: a frontier of states, expanded one at a time. This project searches the space of plans with a genetic algorithm: a population of whole action sequences that is improved by selection, crossover and mutation. The environment, the cost function and the heuristics are shared with the other two projects (rules in Assignment 1’s Rules tab). The project brief describes the algorithm in seven steps; this page answers them in order, and the full reasoning is in the design notes.

    Step 1 · The problem

    The Sandbox of Assignment 1: a tower of n×2 boxes with sand, spring-loaded top-down gates and side gates, action costs 1 + 4·(s + s′) and 1, goal “no sand left”. The task is to find a successful path, a sequence of actions from the initial state to a goal state, and among successful paths a cheap one. The classes are Problem (start state, goal test, actions, length bound, conflict function, large_number), Environment (applies actions, reports cost and legality, replays a sequence) and SolverAgent (the algorithm), all in js/core/genetic.js.

    Step 2 · A state is a sequence of actions

    An individual is an array of action ids read from the start state (S2 L1 R4 …). Its length is bounded by maxLength, 4 actions per row by default (20 for the 5×2 grid; no optimal 5×2 plan needs more than 18). As the brief says, if the goal is reached before the last action the rest of the sequence is ignored: the replay stops there and the later actions are neither applied nor paid for.

    Step 3 · Path_Goal_Test

    successfulPath(problem, env, sequence), also exported as pathGoalTest and successfulPathTest: replay the sequence from the initial state and return true if a goal state is reached at any point, false otherwise.

    Step 4 · Conflict Number

    A function that scores a state by how far it seems from the goal, 0 at the goal. The sequence is replayed from the initial state, every state after an action is scored, and the smallest score seen along the sequence is the conflict number of the sequence, exactly as the brief asks (the end-state value is kept as an option for comparison). The default count is the number of closed top-down gates that still have sand above them in their column, each of which must be opened by one paid action. The brief’s own example, the number of boxes that still hold sand, and six further choices (half-units of sand, the fall distance h₃, the regression estimate h₄, the cost of the greedy approach from the state, the admissible h₂, none) are available and compared.

    Why not the best A* heuristic? Because the fitness below subtracts the conflict number, and a conflict number that underestimates the remaining cost makes an unfinished path look better than its own completion (step 5). The count of gates is multiplied by the largest action cost, 9, and 9 · gates ≥ h* on every one of the 326,041 reachable 5×2 states, so finishing always pays.

    Step 5 · Fitness

    Form. The brief’s general form fitness = large_number − w₁·f(conflict_number) − w₂·g(path_cost), with f and g increasing (identity, √x or ln(1 + x)) and w₁, w₂ positive. Defaults: f = g = identity, w₂ = 1, w₁ = 9 for the gate count. A sequence with an illegal action scores 0. For a successful path the conflict number is 0 and the fitness is large_number − w₂·g(path_cost): successful paths beat unsuccessful ones, and cheaper successful paths beat dearer ones, which are the brief’s two criteria. For an unfinished path the fitness is not zero, as the brief requires: it falls with the conflict number.

    Why the conflict term must be pessimistic. Let a path end in state s with cost g. Its best completion costs g + h*(s) and scores L − g − h*(s); the unfinished path scores L − g − w₁·c(s). If w₁·c(s) < h*(s) the unfinished path is fitter than every way of finishing it, and the fittest individual of all is the empty path. With the admissible h₂ of Assignment 2 used unweighted this is what happens: the fittest individual is a short unfinished path, goal paths survive only as by-products of the random walks, and the plans returned are no better than the greedy approach’s. The smallest-along-the-sequence rule weakens the penalty further (it is at most the end-state value), but the same argument applies to the prefix where the minimum occurs: that prefix, completed optimally, still dominates the whole path.

    large_number. Not chosen by hand: L = 2 · maxLength · maxStepCost + 1 (361 for 20 actions of at most 9). A legal path costs at most maxLength · maxStepCost, the weighted conflict term is a remaining-cost estimate bounded the same way, so every legal path has a positive fitness, which roulette selection needs. The brief treats L as a hyperparameter; it is irrelevant to the ranking but not to roulette (step 7).

    Other forms. Strict (0 unless the goal is reached) is the literal reading of the earlier brief; it is no longer required and gives selection nothing to prefer among unfinished paths. Ratio (L ÷ (1 + w₁·f(c) + w₂·g(cost))) keeps the ranking with compressed differences. Both are measured in the Evaluation tab.

    Step 6 · The genetic algorithm

    6.1 Initial population. population individuals (60 by default), each a random sequence of maxLength actions from the initial state. In this puzzle most actions are illegal in most states (a top-down gate only opens under a box that holds sand and is not already open; a side gate only between a full box and an empty one), so a uniformly random sequence is almost never legal. The initial sequences are therefore random legal walks: at every step a legal action is drawn uniformly. This is what rejection sampling of random sequences would converge to, at a fraction of the cost.

    6.2a Mutation. One random edit, then the part after it is replayed. The three mutations of the brief: replace one action by another, swap two positions, reverse the segment between two positions. Three additions that change the length: insert, delete, cut and regrow with a fresh random walk (the large jump out of a local optimum). All six are enabled by default and can be switched off one by one; the evaluation compares the brief’s three alone.

    Illegal children. The brief’s rule is the lethal mutation: a child with an illegal action is discarded and the operator is drawn again. Three behaviours are implemented and compared. Repair (default): the first action that is illegal in the state it now meets is replaced by a random legal one and the replay continues, so every individual stays legal. Retry (lethal, as in the brief): redraw the operator up to 20 times, then keep the parent so the population size stays constant. Keep with fitness 0: the baseline that shows why this matters.

    6.2b Crossover. Single-point (default): child 1 = prefix of A + suffix of B, child 2 = prefix of B + suffix of A, with independent cut points because the parents differ in length; the prefix is kept intact, so what the first parent achieved up to the cut is inherited exactly, and the suffix inherits the second parent’s intention (which gates, in which order), repaired only where it no longer fits. Double-point: the segments between two cut points are exchanged. The population size stays constant: two parents give two children.

    Step 7 · Selection

    selection(population, n, method) turns fitness into a probability and draws n individuals with replacement: the mating pool, a list of sequences with repeats, returned sorted by fitness. Methods: the textbook roulette pᵢ = fᵢ / Σf; the same wheel after subtracting the worst fitness (default), because with large_number inside every fitness the plain wheel is almost uniform; linear ranking; and a tournament of three. The pool is shuffled and mated in pairs (i, i+1) with crossover probability 0.9; each child is mutated with probability 0.3; the two fittest individuals are copied unchanged (elitism, our addition, can be set to 0). The run stops after 150 generations or 50 without improvement.

    What the evaluation shows

    As the brief asks: 100 random problems with different initial states, the GA against A* and UCS — how many it solves, how many of those solutions are optimal, and the mean of GA_Path_cost ÷ optimum_path_cost over all 100 and over the successes — for the default configuration and for every alternative above, on the 5×2 and the 6×2 grid. A larger run over all 59,049 initial states of the 5×2 grid, made with the first version of the algorithm, is kept below it. See the Evaluation tab.
