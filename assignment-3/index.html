    What this project adds

    Assignments 1 and 2 searched the state space: a frontier of states, expanded one at a time. This project searches the space of plans with a genetic algorithm: a population of whole action sequences that is improved by selection, crossover and mutation. The environment, the cost function and the heuristics are shared with the other two projects (rules in Assignment 1’s Rules tab).

    Legality first

    The brief says a sequence with an illegal action gets fitness 0. In this puzzle most actions are illegal in most states: a top-down gate only opens under a box that holds sand and is not already open, a side gate only between a full box and an empty one. A uniformly random sequence of 20 actions is therefore almost never legal, and splicing two legal sequences or changing one action in the middle breaks the rest of the sequence, because every later action was legal in the state it used to be applied in.

    So the operators are built around legality. The initial population is made of random walks through the environment, which are legal by construction. After a crossover or a mutation the new sequence is replayed from the cut; the first action that is illegal in its new state is replaced by a random legal one, and so on to the end (SolverAgent.build). The same pass cuts a sequence at the first goal state, so path_cost only counts what was needed, and at maxLength. The Evaluation tab has the ablation: with repair switched off (random action ids, illegal actions left in place) the algorithm reaches the goal on 644 of the 59,049 states, 1.1%; 470 of those have an optimal plan of at most seven actions and 596 of at most nine, where a short random sequence can happen to be legal.

    The fitness function

    Default form. fitness = large_number − weight · conflict_numbers − path_cost, and 0 if an action of the sequence is illegal. For a path that reaches the goal conflict_numbers is 0 and the fitness is exactly large_number − path_cost, which is what the brief asks for at the goal.

    Why a partial path must be penalised at least by what it still costs. Let a path end in state s with cost g. The best plan that continues it costs g + h*(s), so its fitness is L − g − h*(s). The partial path itself scores L − g − w·c(s). If w·c(s) < h*(s) the unfinished path is fitter than every way of finishing it, and the fittest individual of all is the empty path. This is exactly what happens with the admissible h₂ of Assignment 2 used unweighted (c = h₂, w = 1): the fittest individual is a short unfinished path, goal paths survive only as by-products, and the plans it returns are no better than the greedy approach (optimal on 7.1% of states, mean ratio 1.173 against 1.066 for the default). The conflict estimate must be pessimistic, the opposite of what A* wants.

    conflict_numbers. The default counts the closed top-down gates that still have sand above them in their column, each of which has to be opened by one paid action. Multiplying the count by the largest possible action cost, 9, gives 9 · gates ≥ h* on every one of the 326,041 reachable states of the 5×2 grid (the largest ratio h* / (3 · gates) is 2.73, below 3), so finishing a path always pays. The alternatives — the sand left in the grid, the fall distance h₃, the regression estimate h₄, the cost of the greedy approach from the state, and the unweighted h₂ — are compared in the Evaluation tab.

    large_number. Not chosen by hand: L = 2 · maxLength · maxStepCost + 1 (361 for 20 actions of at most 9 each). A legal path costs at most maxLength · maxStepCost, the weighted conflict term is a remaining-cost estimate bounded the same way, so every legal path has a positive fitness and roulette selection is well defined. The constant is irrelevant to the ranking but not to roulette: see selection below.

    Other forms. The brief allows a change of form with a justification. Strict (L − path_cost at the goal, 0 otherwise) is the literal reading: it works here only because random legal walks reach the goal often, and it gives selection nothing to prefer among unfinished paths. Ratio (L ÷ (1 + w·c + cost)) keeps the ranking but compresses the differences; the expected loss for fitness-proportional selection did not show up in the measurements (27.4% optimal against 26.9%), most likely because the default wheel subtracts the worst fitness first, which undoes much of the compression. Subtraction is kept as the default because it leaves the fitness differences equal to the cost differences, the quantity the brief's formula names.

    Selection

    Fitness is turned into a probability and n parents are drawn with replacement; the list comes back sorted by fitness, as asked. The textbook wheel pᵢ = fᵢ / Σf has a flaw here that is a direct consequence of large_number: every fitness is roughly L − 50, so the best and the worst individual differ by a few percent and the wheel is almost uniform. Subtracting the worst fitness first (pᵢ ∝ fᵢ − f_min + 1) cancels the constant, leaves the cost differences, and is the default. Linear ranking and a tournament of three are included for comparison; two elites are copied unchanged into the next generation.

    Crossover and mutation

    Crossover is one-point with independent cut points, because the parents differ in length: child 1 = head of A + tail of B, child 2 = head of B + tail of A, each repaired from the cut. The head is kept intact, so what the first parent achieved up to the cut is inherited exactly; the tail inherits the intention of the second parent (which gates it opens, in what order) and is repaired only where that no longer fits.

    Mutation applies one of four edits at a random position and repairs what follows: replace an action by another legal one, insert a legal action, delete an action, or cut the sequence and regrow it with a fresh random walk. The first three are the smallest possible changes to a sequence, the moves of edit distance; the fourth is the large jump that lets the population leave a local optimum.

    What the evaluation shows

    Every variant is run on all 59,049 initial states of the 5×2 grid, with the same seed per state, and compared with the optimum from value iteration: how often it reaches the goal, how often its plan is optimal, the distribution of GA_opt_ratio = GA cost ÷ optimum, the two scatter plots asked for in the first assignment, the number of fitness evaluations and the time. The Evaluation tab draws it all.
