    The problem

    A tower of boxes, n rows by 2 columns. Each box is empty, half full or full of sand. Neighbouring boxes are separated by spring-loaded gates: one top-down gate under every box (the gates under the bottom row are outlets to the outside) and one side gate between the two boxes of every row. A gate stays shut until you open it; it closes again as soon as the sand has settled.

    Goal: reach a state in which no box contains sand, paying as little as possible.

    Initial state

    Every box is drawn independently: empty with probability 0.5, full with 0.3, half full with 0.2. A 5×2 grid therefore has 310 = 59,049 possible initial states.

    Action a — top-down gate

    • Sand falls from the upper box into the lower one until the lower box is full or the upper box is empty. Through an outlet, the whole box drains.
    • Doors pressed from both sides: a spring door opens by itself only when material presses on it from both sides, that is, the box above it holds sand and the box below it is full (touching the door from underneath). So when you open the gate under a full box, the door on top of that box opens too; the door above the next box opens as well if that box was also full, and so on up the pile. A half-full box does not touch the door above it, so nothing above it follows.
    • All these doors are open at the same moment and all the sand falls together.
    • Cost = 1 + 4·(s + s′), where s is the level of the upper box before the action and s′ its level after everything has fallen (0, ½ or 1).

    Action b — side gate

    • Sand levels out only when one box is full and the other is empty: both end up half full. Any other pair would need a level that is not 0, ½ or 1, so nothing moves.
    • Cost = 1.

    Gates that would move nothing are not offered as actions.

    The greedy approach

    “Greedy” in this assignment is not greedy best-first search (that one needs a heuristic). It is the simple procedure proposed in class:

    • never open a side gate; empty the two columns independently;
    • in a column, go to the topmost box that holds sand and open the gate under it; whether the sand moved down or stayed stuck, move on to the box below and do the same, down to the outlet;
    • repeat until the column is empty.

    With a cost of one unit per gate this procedure uses the fewest possible actions. With the cost function above it usually does not give the cheapest plan — which is exactly what the histogram of Greedy_opt_ratio in the Analysis tab measures.

    Why the cost function is interesting

    Flushing a full box while more sand rests on it costs 9 instead of 5, because the box is full again afterwards (s′ = 1). Half boxes are cheap to move (3) and cheap to flush, and a side gate turns one full box into two halves for just 1 — but halves break the chain of pressed doors, so a pile with a half box in the middle no longer drops in one go. The cheapest plan balances the two.

That makes 54.

    The true optimum is nevertheless 45, by a different plan: open the side gate of the top row. The two half boxes it creates are cheap to bring down, and the final flushes of both columns then cost 7 and 3 instead of 9 and 5.

    Proposed extension: water and concrete

    Boxes may also start with water (half or full). Water obeys the same gate rules as sand, with one addition:

    • Whenever sand flows into a box holding water, or water into a box holding sand, the two set into concrete. The box is blocked for good: nothing enters, leaves or passes through it.
    • Through a top-down gate this happens when the lower box has room. Through a side gate it happens when a full box faces a half-full box of the other material: half a box spills over and sets.
    • Every box that turns to concrete adds 10 to the cost. The search does not stop there.
    • The goal is unchanged: no box contains sand. Water and concrete may remain, so pouring water on trapped sand is a legitimate, if expensive, way to get rid of it.

    Some grids become unsolvable (sand stuck above concrete with no water left to reach it); the solver reports those.

    Algorithms and heuristics

    The greedy approach and the four main searches (BFS, DFS, IDS, UCS) are the required part; the informed searches (A*, RBFS and the others) are the bonus part and need a heuristic designed for this problem.

    Memory is counted in states held at once (frontier plus explored set or transposition table). The iterative-deepening variants keep a per-iteration transposition table: without one, the freedom to interleave independent moves in the two columns makes their search trees explode.
